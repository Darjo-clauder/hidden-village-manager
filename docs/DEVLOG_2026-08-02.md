## Systems that look finished and do nothing

The alliance work ended on a note I keep turning over. The pact system was
complete — three types, live benefits, standing, dissolution, all unit-tested,
all rendering — and it was also, for thirty-four months of a driven game,
completely inert. The obligation roll was gated behind `pendingQuickDecision`
and `pendingChoiceEvent`, and those are pending most months. A trade compact ran
nearly three in-game years without invoking once.

Every test passed. The UI was correct. The pure module was correct. What was
wrong was a single clause of scheduling, and nothing short of playing it at
pace would have shown that.

This is the second time in this arc the same shape has appeared. `v.allied` was
a boolean that had been sitting in the codebase doing nothing for months. The
dynasty handoff wrote a record that nothing ever read. In all three cases the
code was *present*, *plausible*, and *inconsequential* — and in all three the
tell was the same: no test could fail, because there was nothing downstream
asserting the thing had an effect.

I don't have a general fix for that. The closest thing I have is the habit of
asking, for any system I touch: what would break if this silently became a
no-op? If the honest answer is "nothing", the system doesn't exist yet,
whatever the diff says.

## The month clock, and an invariant living in the wrong layer

The most consequential thing I got wrong this arc wasn't in a system at all. It
was that I twice reported "N months driven in browser" when the game had never
left Year 1.

`continueTurn()` refuses to advance while a blocking decision is outstanding and
routes the player to it instead. `endTurn()` does not — it calls the tick
directly. Both are bound to `window`. I was driving QA through the guarded one,
watching most of my calls quietly no-op, and reading the result as progress. I
only caught it because achievements failed to unlock and the topbar still read
`Y1 · Snow Moon`.

Tracing it properly afterwards, the structure is worth naming:

```
continueTurn()   ui.js      — checks _pendingState(), routes, then delegates
  └─ endTurn()   room.js    — calls _adv(), saves, syncs
       └─ adv()  adv.js     — the tick itself, no guard of any kind
```

The invariant *you cannot advance the month with an unresolved blocking
decision* is enforced entirely in the presentation layer. The simulation has no
opinion about it. Any caller that reaches `endTurn` — my QA harness, an inline
`onclick`, multiplayer's ready/advance path — steps straight past it.

That has been fine so far because the UI is the only real caller. It stops being
fine the moment turn advancement has a second driver, and multiplayer already
half-gestures at one: `voteAdvance()` emits to the socket, and `setAdvFn()`
injects the tick function into `room.js` from outside. The wiring for a
server-driven tick is most of the way there.

The fix I'd want is not to move the guard — the routing genuinely belongs in the
UI, since it decides *where to send the player*. It's to make `adv()` itself
refuse, or at minimum assert, when it's entered with a blocking decision
outstanding. The tick should not be able to run over the top of an unanswered
council mandate, a pact invocation, or a world choice just because someone
called the wrong function. Right now it will, cheerfully, and the only symptom
is state that quietly diverges from what the player was shown.

This is also the first thing I'd want covered by the tick harness, because it's
exactly the kind of invariant that harness can now assert cheaply.

## A counter that reads last month's dead

Extracting `worldPassives` turned up a coupling I didn't appreciate until I went
looking for it today.

`G._kiaThisMonth` is incremented at five sites scattered across the tick — squad
missions, solo missions, the black-market branch, a transfer departure, village
defence. It is read and zeroed in exactly one place: the citizen-morale block,
which is now in `tick/worldPassives.js` and runs near the *top* of the tick.

Every one of those five write sites is downstream of the read.

So civilian morale each month reacts to the previous month's casualties, then
clears the counter, and this month's deaths accumulate into it for next time.
There is a one-month lag baked into the ordering. It is almost certainly
unintentional — nothing in the code acknowledges it — and it is also, I think,
fine. Public mood trailing the news by a month is defensible, arguably more
defensible than instant reaction.

What concerns me isn't the lag. It's that the lag used to be visible. When all
of this lived in one 2,900-line function you could, in principle, scroll from
the read to the writes. Now the read lives in one file and the writers live in
another, and nothing connects them but a property name on a global. The
characterisation snapshots held byte-identical through the extraction, which
proves I preserved the behaviour — but they'd equally hold if a future
extraction moved mission resolution *above* `worldPassives` and turned the lag
into instant reaction, because the snapshots pin what the sim produces for four
fixed seeds, not why.

The honest read: I made the code more modular and the coupling less legible at
the same time. That's a real cost of the extraction, not a free win, and the
mitigation is documentation at both ends rather than cleverness.

## Mission resolution: why it's the hard one

Lines 1266–1660. Roughly 395 lines, the largest remaining block, and the one I
have been quietly routing around.

The numbers say why. The three blocks already lifted needed almost nothing from
the tick's preamble — `staff` used **zero** of the ten derived locals,
`worldPassives` three, `finance` two plus one straggler (`sponsorshipIncome`)
that failed loudly the moment I ran the tests.

Mission resolution uses **eight of the ten**: `hL`, `dp`, `sb`, `iB`, `cp`,
`clP`, `shP`, `season`. It declares roughly seventy locals of its own. And it is
not a leaf — it is where shinobi die. It pushes to the memorial, increments the
KIA counter that citizen morale reads, feeds the mandate tracker that decides
whether the council keeps you, fires bond ripples across the roster, and writes
the mission report the battle viewer later replays.

Extracting it isn't a matter of moving text. Every one of those is an ordering
dependency with something else in the tick, and the ones I can enumerate are the
ones I already know about.

The procedure that worked three times — contiguous block, check locals don't
leak forward, pass the preamble in as explicit context, run the free-variable
scanner, confirm snapshots byte-identical — is still the right procedure. But it
was calibrated on blocks with two or three dependencies. At eight I'd want to
split the block before moving it: resolve-and-report separated from
apply-consequences, so the part that computes outcomes can move independently of
the part that mutates half the game state.

And I'd want more than four seeds under it first. Four was enough to catch a
missing import in `finance.js` immediately. It was *not* enough to catch
`addLegend` and `addMemory` being unimported in `staff.js` — those sat green
because no seed happened to graduate a mentorship or form a mentor bond. A
latent crash on a live path, caught only by a static scan I wrote afterwards.
Mission resolution has far more branches than staff does, and most of them are
rare by design: KIA, trauma, bond ripple, clan reaction. Seeded coverage is
exactly the wrong tool for rare branches.

## The academy, which is easier and riskier than it looks

By the coupling metric the youth academy is the best remaining candidate. The
development tick (2087–2247) uses **zero** of the preamble locals. It touches
its own state almost exclusively — `intakeClass`, `academyRecords`,
`gradTracking`, `youthCupHistory`. On paper it lifts as cleanly as `staff` did.

The risk is somewhere else entirely, and it's the same risk as the KIA counter:
*when* it runs.

The academy is the roster's supply line. Intake lands in April, walk-ins in
October, the Youth Cup in June, and graduation moves students out of
`intakeClass` and into the prospect pool mid-tick. Meanwhile a separate block
enforces the roster floor by auto-signing the best available prospect whenever
the roster falls below fourteen — a rule that exists precisely because mission
KIA can empty a village faster than the academy fills it.

So there is an implicit chain every month: missions kill people → the floor
check looks for replacements → the academy determines who is available to be
found. Move the academy across the floor check and you have changed how the
game recovers from a bad year, in a way that will not show up as an error and
may not show up in a four-seed snapshot either. It would show up as a slow drift
in roster stability across a long dynasty — the hardest class of regression to
attribute.

That's the argument for doing the academy *before* mission resolution, not
after. It's the smaller change, it exercises the ordering question in a place
where I can reason about the whole chain, and if the snapshots do move I'll know
the cause is ordering rather than one of seventy locals.

## Onboarding, and systems keyed to the wrong clock

A smaller thread, but it rhymes with the rest.

The old first-year guidance rendered for Y1 M1–4. The council sets mandates each
January and grades them each December, and two bad years running is a dismissal.
The guidance therefore disappeared eight months before the player's first
review, on a system that can end their run. Nobody chose that. Someone picked
"the first few months" as the window for onboarding, which is a reasonable
instinct, and the mandate system arrived later with its own clock, and the two
were never reconciled.

That's the same failure as the KIA lag and the obligation gate: two subsystems,
each internally coherent, with an unstated assumption about timing between them.
None of the three was a bug in the sense of a wrong line. All three were
scheduling.

I'm increasingly convinced that for a simulation this size, the interesting
defects are almost never inside a system. They're in the seams — what runs
before what, what reads a value the frame after it's written, what window a
piece of UI assumes the player is in. The type ramp and the elevation pass were
satisfying because they were *legibly* wrong and mechanically fixable. The
scheduling defects took playing the thing at pace, and I found each of them by
accident.

## What's next, in order

1. **Assert the turn invariant in the tick.** `adv()` should refuse to run with a
   blocking decision outstanding, and the harness should have a test that proves
   it. This is small, and it closes the hole I fell into myself.
2. **Widen seeded coverage** before any further extraction — more seeds, and
   ideally a mode that forces rare branches rather than waiting for them.
3. **Extract the youth academy**, deliberately, watching the roster-floor
   interaction, as a rehearsal for the ordering questions.
4. **Split mission resolution** into compute and apply, then extract the compute
   half.
5. Leave the per-shinobi tick and people-management for last; they're the most
   entangled with the roster and the least urgent.

The through-line for all of it: the simulation loop is stabilising, but its
remaining risk has moved from *what the code does* to *when it does it*. The
test net catches the first category well. It barely touches the second. That's
the gap worth closing next.
